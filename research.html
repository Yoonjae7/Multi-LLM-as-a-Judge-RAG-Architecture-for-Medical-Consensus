Building a Multi-LLM-as-a-Judge RAG System for Evidence-Grounded Western Medicine

Yoonjae Lee

School of Computer Science, University of Nottingham Malaysia

BSc (Hons) Computer Science with Artificial Intelligence

Supervisor: Dr Simon Lau Boung Yew

MediRAG-West Research report Not a medical device

Summary

Language models answer health questions fluently but unreliably. They state things confidently without a source, they give one answer where a clinician would weigh several considerations, and they rarely signal when the evidence behind an answer is thin.

This report describes MediRAG-West, the Western medicine part of our MediRAG-Judge system. A question goes to a query planner, then to a retrieval step that pulls real clinical evidence, then to three specialist agents that answer independently from that same evidence. Four judges score those answers for evidence quality, safety, conflict and confidence, and only then is a single answer produced, carrying a confidence score, its citations, and any disagreement the agents did not resolve.

The report makes two arguments. First, that you should pick a different model for each role rather than using the best available model everywhere, and that the standard medical benchmark, MedQA, is no longer useful for making that choice. Second, that refusing to answer has to be designed in, because a system that cannot decline cannot be trusted when it does not decline.

1The problem

Health questions are among the most common things people ask a language model, and among the worst suited to how language models answer. Ask about chest tightness on exertion and you get a well-formed paragraph whether or not the model has any grounding for what is in it. The failure is rarely a wrong fact stated plainly. It is a plausible fact stated with unearned confidence, without a citation, and without any hint that the model is at the edge of what it knows.

Four problems come up repeatedly in the literature, and they shape everything below.

  • Made-up facts. Medical hallucination is measurable and persistent. The Med-HALT benchmark[1] builds tests from medical exam data across several countries and finds large differences between models in how readily they assert things they cannot support. The problem does not go away at frontier scale.
  • No visible evidence. A fluent answer with no traceable source cannot be checked by the person reading it. Retrieval-augmented generation[2] fixes this in principle, but only if the generated text is genuinely constrained to what was retrieved rather than just displayed next to it.
  • One opaque answer. Clinical reasoning weighs competing considerations. Showing a single merged answer throws away the disagreement, which is often the most informative part, because disagreement is what signals uncertainty.
  • Safety left to chance. Emergencies and drug interactions need predictable handling. A system that relies on the generating model to notice that a question describes a stroke has already made an unsafe design choice.

Each has a known partial fix. Retrieval grounds claims in real sources[2][3], having several agents debate improves factual accuracy over a single model[4], models used as judges agree with human evaluators reasonably well[5], and giving a model an explicit option to decline makes it decline more appropriately[6]. The open question this report addresses is how to combine them into one pipeline without the combination itself becoming the source of error, and which model belongs in which position.

Scope

This report covers the Western medicine module only. The Traditional Chinese Medicine module, the reconciliation between the two traditions, and the combined dual-medicine answer are outside its scope and are not claimed as built.

2What others have done

Retrieval in medicine

Retrieval-augmented generation[2] feeds the model documents fetched at the moment of asking, which separates factual content from what is baked into the model weights. In medicine that matters twice over, because clinical guidance changes continuously and every claim needs a source someone can look up.

The strongest evidence comes from Xiong et al.[3], who built MIRAGE, a benchmark of 7,663 medical questions, and tested 41 combinations of corpus, retriever and model. They report accuracy gains of up to 18 percentage points over standard prompting, enough to lift a mid-tier model to the level of a much stronger model used without retrieval. They also found that more retrieved text is not simply better, and reproduced the "lost in the middle" effect[7], where evidence placed in the centre of a long context gets used less reliably than evidence at either end. Both findings shaped our retrieval design.

Clinically, Almanac[8] is the strongest demonstration so far. It was tested against standard models on 314 clinical questions across nine specialties, scored by board-certified clinicians, and showed significant gains in factuality. It establishes that retrieval produces quality differences clinicians can actually see, not just benchmark differences.

Several agents instead of one

Du et al.[4] showed that having several model instances propose answers and then debate them improves factual accuracy and reduces hallucination. In medicine specifically, MedAgents[9] assigns role-played domain experts to a multi-round discussion without any fine-tuning, and MDAgents[10] adds the useful idea that the collaboration structure should adapt to how hard the question is. MDAgents reports best performance on seven of ten benchmarks and an average accuracy gain of 11.8% for group work with a moderator and outside knowledge.

The counter-evidence deserves equal weight. Multi-agent systems are not free, and recent work questions whether they beat a well-tuned single agent once you account for the cost[11]. Our view is that in this domain the ensemble earns its place less through accuracy than through visibility. The disagreement between agents is itself a safety signal we want to show the user, and in a single-agent design that signal does not exist at all.

Models as judges

Zheng et al.[5] established the reference result. Strong models used as judges reach about 85% agreement with human evaluators, which is higher than the roughly 81% agreement humans reach with each other. The same work catalogues the failure modes any judge design has to defend against: preferring whichever answer came first, preferring longer answers, and preferring their own output.

Later large-scale audits are more cautious. Measured properly, agreement scores overstate how well judges actually discriminate, judge rankings shift between benchmarks, and a judge can be highly consistent while still being badly biased by ordering[12]. A judge that is consistent is not therefore correct. This is why our judges score against written rubrics rather than giving free-form opinions.

For health specifically, HealthBench[13] is the most relevant instrument: 5,000 multi-turn health conversations scored against 48,562 criteria written by 262 physicians, covering things like emergency referral and behaviour under uncertainty. Its themes map almost directly onto what our judges score.

3Choosing a model for each role

The usual approach is to pick the highest-scoring model available and use it everywhere. That is expensive, and we think it is also wrong. The pipeline contains jobs with genuinely different requirements, and for some of them the strongest model is not the right choice.

The benchmarks are not interchangeable

A benchmark score is only useful if the benchmark measures the thing the job needs. Medical evaluation contains instruments that measure quite different things, and treating them as one number leads to bad decisions.

BenchmarkSizeWhat it measures
MedQA[14] 12,723 items US licensing exam recall and reasoning, multiple choice. Now saturated.
MedXpertQA[15] 4,460 items Expert-level reasoning across 17 specialties, built specifically because MedQA got too easy.
HealthBench[13] 5,000 conversations Realistic multi-turn dialogue scored against physician rubrics. Closest to how the system is actually used.
Med-HALT[1] Varies How readily a model asserts things it cannot support.
MIRAGE[3] 7,663 questions Performance with retrieval switched on, which is the setting we actually deploy in.
MedQA is no longer a useful test

Med-PaLM 2 reported 86.5% and GPT-4 with Medprompt reported 90.2%[16][17], and current leaderboards place several models above 94%. When the gap between candidate models is smaller than the benchmark's own noise, the benchmark cannot rank them. Selection should be driven by HealthBench, MedXpertQA and Med-HALT instead.

One more useful data point. Recent evaluation in Nature Medicine finds that general-purpose frontier models outperform specialised clinical AI tools[18]. For us that is good news, because it means the leverage is in the system design, that is in retrieval, judging and knowing when to decline, rather than in obtaining a specially trained medical model we could not train anyway.

What goes where

A three-agent, four-judge pipeline makes roughly ten model calls per question, so using a frontier model for all of them costs about ten times a single-call baseline. That is unnecessary for most of the jobs. The table below uses one current model family as a worked example, because its per-tier pricing is published, but the reasoning is not tied to any vendor.

JobTierWhy
Emergency check Cheapest This runs as a fixed list of warning signs first, with a small model as a second pass to catch wordings the list misses. The guarantee comes from the rule list being auditable and tuned to catch too much rather than too little, not from model capability. A frontier model here would add cost and delay to a decision that needs to be predictable and repeatable.
Query planner Mid Breaking a question into search terms under a fixed format. Following instructions matters more than deep reasoning.
Retrieval Not a chat model An embedding model for finding candidates and a cross-encoder for ranking them.
Specialist agents Mid Because answers are constrained to retrieved evidence, a frontier model helps less here than its benchmark scores suggest. MIRAGE shows retrieval lifting mid-tier models to the level of stronger ones[3].
Judges Best The one place we do not economise. Judges are the last check before the user sees anything, judge reliability is fragile[12], and judge quality limits how much any claim can be trusted.
The main point

How capable a model needs to be does not track how bad it is when it fails. The job with the worst failure, missing an emergency, gets the cheapest model, because its reliability comes from the design rather than from the model. The job that most needs a strong model is judging, which is the first place most systems try to save money.

Judges must not be the same model as the agents

Models prefer their own output[5]. That gives one hard rule: no judge should be the same model family as an agent it is scoring. Where that is not possible, judging has to be done blind, with agent identity stripped out and the order of answers shuffled each time. We also score the four axes in separate calls, because scoring them together lets a good evidence score bleed into the safety score, which is exactly the link the panel exists to break.

4How the system works

The pipeline has six steps, matching the architecture diagram on the project home page. Two of them can stop the process early without producing any answer at all.

1 and 2. Question and Query Planner

Before anything else, the planner screens for presentations that need urgent care, such as possible heart or breathing emergencies, stroke signs, severe allergic reactions, uncontrolled bleeding, and risk of self-harm. This happens before any search and before any model generates clinical text. If it triggers, the pipeline stops immediately with a referral to urgent care. Nothing is retrieved and no agent runs, which also removes any chance of the system discussing symptoms in a way that delays care.

If it does not trigger, the planner turns the question into search terms and decides which agents to activate. A question about dietary iron does not need every agent, and running one anyway produces filler rather than insight.

3. MediRAG-West retrieval

Retrieval runs a keyword search and a meaning-based search at the same time, then combines and re-ranks the results.

  • Keyword search using BM25[19]. Essential in medicine, where exact drug names and guideline numbers have to match literally.
  • Meaning-based search for everyday phrasing, where "chest feels tight when I climb stairs" shares almost no words with the clinical text it should match.
  • Combining both with reciprocal rank fusion[20], which merges by position rather than score and so sidesteps the fact that the two searches produce incompatible numbers.
  • Re-ranking the top results with a cross-encoder, which is more accurate because it looks at the question and the document together.
  • Ordering the surviving evidence with the strongest passages first and last, to work around the lost-in-the-middle effect[7].

If nothing clears the relevance threshold, the pipeline stops and says so. This is the most important check in the whole system. It is what stops the model answering from memory when the search fails, which is exactly when unsupported claims appear.

4. Agent debate

The selected agents answer independently and at the same time, each given the same evidence but a different brief. Independence matters, because agents that can see each other's drafts converge too early, and that destroys the disagreement signal the whole design exists to surface.

AgentCovers
Western MedicineWhat the condition may be, red flags, when to seek care, medication cautions
NutritionDietary factors with real mechanistic or trial support
LifestyleSleep, activity, behaviour and stress

Each agent returns claims tagged with the evidence supporting them, a certainty label using GRADE categories[21], and an explicit list of what it could not work out from the evidence given. Claims with no supporting evidence are dropped automatically, rather than being discouraged in the prompt and quietly ignored.

The agents then see each other's claims once and may revise, concede, or hold their position. We limit this to a single round, because more rounds mostly produce agreement rather than correction, and agreement is the thing we are trying not to manufacture. Whatever they still disagree about is carried through to the final answer rather than smoothed away.

5. SafeJudge

Four judges score the revised answers on separate axes, in separate calls, with agent identity removed and answer order shuffled.

JudgeAsks
Evidence JudgeIs every claim traceable to retrieved evidence, and are the citations accurate?
Safety JudgeAny contraindications, interactions, missed red flags, or advice that could delay care?
Conflict JudgeDo the agents really disagree, or is it just a difference in emphasis?
Confidence JudgeIs the certainty being expressed proportionate to the evidence behind it?
The Safety Judge blocks, it does not vote

The Safety Judge does not contribute a score that gets averaged in. If it objects, the answer is thrown out and replaced with a cautious safety response, no matter how well the other three scored it. Averaging safety into a combined score would let a well-cited, internally consistent, unsafe answer through, which is the exact failure this panel exists to prevent.

6. Final answer

The last step combines the surviving claims, the judge scores and the remaining disagreement into what the user sees: a plain-language summary, citations, a confidence score, safety notes, and a statement of what the agents did not agree on. Claims the Evidence Judge flagged as unsupported are excluded, and this step is not given the chance to rescue them.

5Confidence and safety

What the confidence score means

The confidence figure has to reflect evidence quality, not just how much the models agreed. Models trained on overlapping data agree for reasons that have nothing to do with being right, so agreement on its own is a weak and potentially misleading signal. We combine three things:

  • Evidence coverage, how well the surviving claims are supported by retrieved sources, weighted by how relevant those sources were.
  • Agreement between agents, counted only over claims that address the same point, so that agents answering different sub-questions are not scored as disagreeing.
  • Judge scores from the evidence, conflict and confidence judges.

Evidence and judge scores each carry roughly twice the weight of agreement, deliberately. Treating agreement between similar models as independent confirmation is the statistical mistake that makes ensembles look more reliable than they are. On top of that, several penalties reduce the result: sources that still need human review, claims resting on a single source, and low-certainty evidence categories. These multiply rather than subtract, so several separate weaknesses compound. The score is always shown next to what it is made of, never on its own.

Declining is a designed outcome

Giving a model an explicit way to decline measurably improves how appropriately it declines[6]. The system therefore has named end states, each with its own response:

StateWhenWhat the user gets
Answer givenEvidence found, nothing blockedFull grounded response
Safety stopEmergency signs, or the Safety Judge objectsUrgent care referral only
Out of scopeNot something this module coversA decline, with a redirection
Need more detailQuestion too vague to search againstClarifying questions
No evidenceNothing relevant enough was foundNothing. It says so and stops.

The last state is what separates this from an ordinary assistant. When the search fails, the system says so and stops. It does not fall back on what the model happens to remember, because that fallback is indistinguishable, to the person reading it, from a properly grounded answer.

Where this sits legally

The system provides health information, not clinical decision support. It does not take in patient medical records, does not produce patient-specific diagnoses or treatment plans, and shows the basis for every statement so a reader can check it independently. These properties line up with the criteria that separate regulated clinical decision support software from software that is not a device[22], and that boundary is a design constraint rather than a disclaimer added at the end.

Hard limits

No doses. No prescriptions. No personalised treatment plan. No instruction to start, stop or change any medication. No diagnosis. These are enforced in the prompts, checked by the Safety Judge, and cannot be overridden by asking.

6How we would test it

Accuracy alone is the wrong target. A system that answers everything at 85% accuracy is worse, here, than one that answers 70% of questions at 92% and declines the rest. So the test plan measures grounding, calibration and declining alongside correctness.

WhatMeasured how
Retrieval qualityRecall and ranking quality against annotated question and passage pairs
GroundingManual audit of whether each claim traces to real evidence, plus how well the Evidence Judge agrees with human raters
Made-up contentRate of unsupported assertions, using Med-HALT style probes[1]
Answer qualityPhysician-written rubrics in the style of HealthBench[13]
SafetyHow many real emergencies are caught, and how many false alarms that costs
CalibrationWhether a stated 80% confidence is right about 80% of the time
DecliningHow often it correctly declines, and how often it declines when it should not
Judge reliabilityAgreement with humans corrected for chance, and how much scores shift when answer order is swapped[12]

The design also makes claims that can be proved wrong, and each should be tested by removing the part in question: no retrieval, a single agent instead of three, no debate round, no judges, no evidence check, and one frontier model used for everything.

The most important single experiment is letting a judge share a model family with the agents it scores. If the scores do not move, our most restrictive design rule is unnecessary and should be dropped. If they do move, it is justified.

7Limitations

Stated plainly

This is a design with a working demonstration interface. The measurements in Section 6 are a plan, not results. Nothing here should be read as evidence that the system performs as intended.

  • No clinical validation. Nothing has been tested against clinical outcomes, and none of it is suitable for care decisions.
  • Small library. The demonstration corpus is tiny next to the medical literature. Gaps show up as the system declining, which is the safe failure, but declining on answerable questions is still a real cost to the user.
  • Judges are models too. Using language models to check language models has a circularity problem that cannot be fully removed. Keeping judges in a different model family and scoring blind reduces it. Human evaluation is still needed.
  • The confidence weights are guesses. They are sensible starting values, not values fitted to real calibration data, and should be tuned before any confidence figure is treated as meaningful.
  • Cost. Roughly ten model calls per question. Whether that beats a well-tuned single agent on accuracy is contested[11], and our justification rests on visibility, which the tests above still have to confirm.
  • Benchmarks are proxies. Doing well on a benchmark does not guarantee doing well in deployment, and contamination is hard to rule out.
  • One language, one jurisdiction. The guidance is mostly UK and US, so how well it applies elsewhere is not established.

8Conclusion

This report described MediRAG-West and argued for a particular way of building it.

The first argument is that model choice should be made job by job, and that how capable a model needs to be does not track how bad it is when it fails. The most safety-critical job here, catching emergencies, gets the cheapest model, because its reliability comes from an auditable list of warning signs rather than from model capability. The job that most needs a strong model is judging, which is where most systems cut costs first. Meanwhile MedQA, still the most cited medical benchmark, has become too easy to separate frontier models, and selection should move to HealthBench, MedXpertQA and hallucination-specific tests.

The second argument is that declining has to be designed in. Two of the six steps can stop the pipeline without producing an answer, and the evidence check in particular is what stops the system quietly substituting model memory when the search fails. A system that cannot decline cannot be trusted when it does not decline.

Both claims can be tested, and Section 6 says how.
